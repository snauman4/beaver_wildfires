Do dam analogs help? Streams above vs below each project

Volunteers with the Coalition for the Poudre River Watershed sample paired stations above and below stream reaches in the Cameron Peak burn, about monthly. If dam analogs improve water, the below station should look better than the above station after the structures go in. This chart lets you check that against the real samples.

Nitrate: how it affects water qualityA form of nitrogen that plants and algae feed on. Burned soils can release extra nitrate and fewer plants remain to take it up, so burn-scar streams can carry more. Too much feeds algae that use up the oxygen fish need and can foul drinking-water supplies (the U.S. drinking-water limit is 10 mg/L as nitrogen). Slow, low-oxygen pond sediment lets bacteria turn nitrate into nitrogen gas, so beaver-style ponds are expected to lower nitrate downstream. That expectation is the hypothesis here, not a finding.
pH: how it affects water qualityA measure of how acidic (below 7) or basic (above 7) the water is. Most stream life does best between about 6.5 and 9, the range used in Colorado and EPA aquatic-life standards. Ash is alkaline and can push burn-scar water up, and low pH can free metals such as aluminum and copper that harm fish. pH also changes how drinking-water treatment works. There is no strong expectation that dam analogs change pH; here it mainly checks whether either the burn or the structures push water outside the usual range.

What this page can and cannot show

This page puts beaver records, the Cameron Peak Fire, and measured river turbidity and flow side by side. It cannot show when beaver dams or dam analogs were added, and it cannot show whether they changed turbidity. The reason is missing data, not a choice: two gaps, described below, block that comparison. Nothing on the page is a made-up value, but several things are interpolated or incomplete, and they are listed here.

1. Beaver and beaver dam analog (BDA) data

  • No installation or arrival dates. The 5 dam records inside the watershed come from expert-mapped imagery datasets and carry no date at all; the mapping covers imagery from roughly 2014 to 2022. A dam on the map shows that one existed at some point in that span, not when it was built.
  • Sightings are not additions. The 64 beaver sightings (GBIF and iNaturalist, 2018 to 2026) are dated by when someone saw an animal. They reflect where people look and photograph, cluster around Fort Collins and Greeley, and say nothing about when a beaver arrived or a dam was built. Dams can also outlast the beavers that built them.
  • Human-built BDAs exist, but are not in the data. The Coalition for the Poudre River Watershed (CPRW) and the Forest Service have built post-fire BDAs and other structures in the burn area. Their project pages give timing only (table below). None gives coordinates, per-structure counts, or a downloadable map, and I did not place any from a place name.
  • No beaver release dataset was found. Colorado Parks and Wildlife publishes guidance on beaver relocation, but I found no record of where or when beavers were released in the Poudre.
  • Verified BDAs are scarce overall. The wider ground-truth database behind this project has only 35 confirmed BDAs, all in Oregon and located only to about 250 m. Colorado has none.

2. Turbidity data

  • No USGS turbidity during the fire. USGS had no turbidity record anywhere in the Poudre watershed in 2020. Its only river turbidity sensors are at Greeley and began in 2026; a Big Thompson sensor ran only in 2017 to 2019, before the fire.
  • The measured turbidity comes from one research network, and it is sparse in 2020. The CSU ROSS sensors that cover the fire were all on the lower river below the canyon. Across the 112 days of the fire window only 1 to 5 of them reported on any day (2 or fewer on 43 of those days), and none were in the burned headwaters or at a beaver site. More sites were added from 2023 on.
  • Data quality is uneven. The providers note that 2019 to 2021 data predate their calibration procedures, and that turbidity sensors foul easily, leaving gaps and doubtful extremes. The sensors are pulled for winter, so there is almost no data from about December to March.
  • The best before-and-after sites are not usable for it. The Little Beaver Creek sensor ran only from late April to early October 2024, ending about when the structures there were finished. The South Fork sensor at Pingree Park Road ran from October 2023 to November 2025, spanning the fall 2024 work, but the project pages do not say whether the structures sit upstream of it.
  • River color is interpolated. Only the diamond markers are actual readings. The color between them is a distance-weighted blend, carried at most 3 km past the last sensor and never up a tributary, so most of the watershed’s streams are uncolored on any given day.
  • Drinking-water intake turbidity is out of reach. Fort Collins Utilities reported post-fire turbidity at its intake, but I could only find it in charts and annual PDF reports, not as downloadable data.
  • Causes cannot be separated. Even with dates and sensors, turbidity here also depends on storms, flow, the burn scar’s recovery, and upstream inputs. A single before-and-after at one site could show a change but not who caused it.

3. Nitrate, pH, and the above-vs-below comparison

  • The comparison exists, but the “after” side is almost empty. CPRW volunteers sample paired stations above and below project reaches about monthly. At Little Beaver Creek there are 22 paired samples before construction (Dec 2021 to Aug 2024) and only 3 after, all from July to September 2025. At the South Fork there are 4 before (May to Aug 2024) and 3 after. At Sheep Creek, built in summer/fall 2025, there are none after: the samples end in September 2025.
  • What the samples show so far is nothing. The median below-minus-above differences are tiny before and after at both sites (nitrate within 0.03 mg/L, well under the 0.35 mg/L gap seen by chance at pairs with no project; pH within 0.09 units, about the size of the 0.07-unit chance gap). With this few samples no conclusion is possible, for or against.
  • The pairs may not bracket the structures. The project pages do not say where, between or beyond the two stations, the dam analogs were placed. The South Fork page gives no location at all.
  • There is no confirmed untreated control. Fish Creek, Lazy D and Ratville have the same above/below design and no project known to me, so they show how much the stations differ by chance. I cannot confirm they were untouched.
  • Seasons are mismatched. The “before” samples cover every season, and the “after” samples are one summer. Nitrate and pH both swing with season and flow, and the burn scar keeps recovering, so a before-and-after at one site cannot isolate the structures.
  • Expectations are not results. That ponds lower nitrate is a widely held expectation from how pond sediments work. Nothing on this page measures it. Nitrate here is as reported by the labs (the form is not stated), 56 non-detect or unreported results were dropped, and the volunteer program’s own quality procedures were not reviewed.

4. What would make the comparison possible

  • More after-construction samples at the same paired stations (a full year, all seasons), and the exact location of each structure relative to the stations.
  • Dates and coordinates for each BDA or beaver release, for example from CPRW or Forest Service as-built maps, or from the researchers’ own field records.
  • Turbidity measured just above and just below the structures, both before and after they went in, ideally with a comparable untreated reach.
  • Raw turbidity records from Fort Collins Utilities and CSU’s post-fire monitoring, which exist but are not in the public data used here.

Bottom line. What you see is real: the fire’s mapped perimeters, documented beaver records, measured turbidity and flow, and paired nitrate and pH samples. What you do not see is a record of when beaver structures arrived or enough after-construction samples, so this page can show you where to look but cannot yet show that dam analogs improved water quality.
